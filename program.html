Workshop. Title: Beyond Interoperability: Hands-On Federated ML for Research Data Curation Infrastructure

Presenter Name: Arnab Mukherjee

Bio: Arnab Mukherjee is a Postdoctoral Researcher at the SAFE Evidence Lab at Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences focused on developing trustworthy, reproducible AI for health research. He earned a PhD in Computer Science with a specialization in Machine Learning from the University of Kansas. His work spans federated AI, machine learning systems, evidence generation, and large-scale biomedical data analysis. He leads the development of scalable ML and LLM pipelines, contributing to formal problem formulation, simulation design, evaluation frameworks, and automated evidence extraction. His research emphasizes open, interoperable, and reproducible machine learning infrastructure that enables transparent and reliable scientific discovery. Looking forward, he aims to advance federated AI systems and open-science platforms that improve the rigor, scalability, and real-world impact of biomedical and clinical research.

Abstract: Interoperability—the ability of systems to securely exchange, interpret, and use data without human intervention—has become a foundation of modern research data curation infrastructure. Yet interoperability alone does not establish whether data from different sites encode equivalent underlying phenomena. When clinical data exhibits a distributional shift across institutions, models trained at one site may fail silently at another. Confident but wrong is the worst possible failure mode, particularly when models are deployed across organizations. This is the foundational curation challenge this workshop addresses. Research software without borders requires not just that data can cross institutional lines, but that what the data means can be trusted on the other side. Site-level measurement heterogeneity determines whether data generated across diverse sources can be meaningfully compared, interpreted, and trusted as part of a shared research infrastructure. We complement interoperability-focused approaches by developing methods to identify and quantify site-level heterogeneity before data and models are integrated, thereby distinguishing true underlying variation from measurement-induced differences and establishing a more reliable foundation for curation in cross-site federated research. This workshop translates that framework into practice. Participants will engage in hands-on Python exercises using conformal prediction under simulated site-level heterogeneity, working directly with federated ML tools designed for research data curation pipelines. Attendees will leave with reusable code, a conceptual framework for distinguishing distributional shift from measurement-induced heterogeneity, and practical uncertainty quantification tools they can deploy within their own cross-institutional curation infrastructure.

Presentation. Title: The RCP Discovery Internship Program: Building a Global Innovation Lab for Research Software

Presenter Name: Rowland Mosbergen

Bio: Rowland is a strategic leader in digital research infrastructure with 25+ years’ experience translating HPC, AI, data and cloud technologies into scalable research and industry services used by global communities. Known for building partnerships across researchers, national infrastructure providers and technology teams to deliver high-impact digital capability, Rowland provides strategic direction, inclusive leadership and scalable programs that accelerate research adoption and demonstrate measurable impact.

Abstract: The RCP Discovery Internship Program at WEHI (Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research) presents a novel model for advancing research software through a globally distributed, fully online internship framework. Since its launch in July 2021, the program has engaged over 300 interns from six continents across 14 intakes, while proving that research software complexity can be addressed efficiently with minimal resources while reducing risk and maximising efficiency and effectiveness.

This presentation describes how the program functions as an innovation lab for interns: recruiting intentionally multi-disciplinary teams, embedding DEI principles, and deploying a “best effort” mentorship philosophy that prioritises critical thinking, adaptability, and tolerance for ambiguity over rigid deliverables.

We outline the operational strategies that enable a single supervisor to manage cohorts of up to 40 interns simultaneously, including structured documentation practices, Individual Learning Plans, Technical Diaries, asynchronous feedback mechanisms, with a focus on guided autonomy.

We also showcase how this program has innovated for researchers, highlighting three key outcomes: Bioconductor package compliance, streamlined flow cytometry workflows via automated file merging, and the REDMANE research data ecosystem that is now available as a demo.

We also highlight how this program has created a new effective methodology for tackling complex software projects that moves from proof-of-concept through to handover, deliberately surfacing complexity early rather than deferring it.

Presentation. Title: Community Is Infrastructure Too: Building Research Software Capacity Through Bioinformatics Training in Africa

Presenter Name: Chioma B. Oselu

Bio: Chioma Oselu is a PhD researcher in Bioinformatics at Covenant University, Nigeria, with research focused on equitable disease risk prediction, reproducible genomic workflows, and research software for underrepresented populations. She is a L’Oréal-UNESCO For Women in Science Laureate for Sub-Saharan Africa and currently serves as President of ISCB Student Council Regional Student Group-Nigeria.

Chioma has trained and mentored over 800 students and researchers through bioinformatics and computational biology workshops, webinars, course design and curriculum development. She has contributed to capacity-building efforts through platforms including Bioconductor, Outreachy, Wellcome Connecting Science, ISCB-SC RSG-Nigeria, African Bioinformatics Institute (ABI) and university-led computational biology initiatives. Through her research, mentorship, and talks, she supports a more transparent and inclusive future for precision medicine, especially for underrepresented populations.

Abstract: When we talk about research infrastructure, we often imagine machines: servers, clusters, cloud platforms, workflow systems. and pipelines. These are important, but in many African bioinformatics spaces, the first infrastructure a learner meets is not a machine. It is a person who helps them understand the error message, read the documentation, find the missing file and try again. This talk reflects on community as a core layer of research software infrastructure. Drawing from my experience supporting bioinformatics training and capacity-building across Africa through community-led webinars, mentorship programmes, practical workshops, curriculum development, and learner support systems, I will explore how people, beyond platforms, sustain research software practice. These experiences include work with ISCB-SC Regional Student Group Nigeria, Bioconductor BugSigDB, Outreachy, Wellcome Connecting Science training, African Bioinformatics Institute (ABI) activities, and computational biology curriculum development.

Using bioinformatics as a case study, the talk will examine how training can move beyond “run this command” into reproducible research software practice. In many training settings, learners are introduced to tools but are not always supported to understand documentation, manage file structures, troubleshoot errors, version their work, organise outputs, or explain their workflows clearly. Yet these are the everyday practices that determine whether research software is reused, trusted, maintained or abandoned.

The central argument is that sustainable research software capacity is not built by tools alone. It also depends on mentoring structures, peer support, documentation habits, local leadership and communities that remain useful after formal workshops end. In resource-limited settings, these human systems often determine whether learners move from passive software users to confident practitioners who can adapt tools to their own research questions.

This presentation will share practical lessons for designing inclusive training programmes, reducing entry barriers for early-career researchers, supporting learners with different levels of prior exposure, and building community structures that strengthen research software use across institutions and regions. It will also reflect on how African research software communities can treat mentorship, peer learning, and local coordination as serious infrastructure for scientific progress. If we want research software to cross borders, then our communities must cross borders too: across institutions, countries, disciplines, and levels of experience. Because in the end, software may run the analysis, but community keeps the work alive.

Presentation. Title: Failure Prediction in AI Agent Pipelines: A Shift Toward More Reliable Research Automation

Presenter Name: Faith Sodipe

Bio: Faith is an experienced backend software engineer with years of experience building distributed systems, data pipelines, and AI-integrated platforms. He works at the intersection of practical engineering and emerging technology, designing systems that are reliable, scalable, and built to last.

Beyond his day job, Faith mentors developers through Codebar and the Women Coding Community, and is an active contributor to the wider tech community through speaking and writing. His current work explores how AI agents behave under real-world conditions, particularly how to anticipate and handle failure before it becomes a problem.

He believes that well-engineered software, clearly communicated, is one of the most powerful tools for closing gaps between disciplines, between regions, and between those who build technology and those who benefit from it.

Abstract: AI agents are showing up everywhere in research software, summarising literature, running experiments, orchestrating data workflows. And for good reason. They reduce toil, speed up iteration, and make complex automation feel almost effortless.

But anyone who has run an AI agent pipeline in production knows the uncomfortable truth: they fail in ways that are surprisingly hard to anticipate, and even harder to debug after the fact. Unlike traditional software, where a broken function throws an exception and points you to a line number, AI agent failures tend to be quiet. A tool call returns something plausible but wrong. A multi-step pipeline drifts off course three steps in. An agent confidently completes a task it fundamentally misunderstood. By the time you notice, the damage is already downstream.

This talk explores what failure in AI agent pipelines actually looks like in practice, not the textbook definitions, but the messy, real-world patterns that emerge when you put these systems under pressure. We’ll look at how to categorise agent failures across planning, tool use, and context management, and more importantly, how to build systems that can see failure coming before it lands.

Drawing on patterns from production engineering and emerging research in agent reliability, we’ll walk through practical approaches to failure prediction: designing observable pipelines, building lightweight failure classifiers, and instrumenting agent behaviour in ways that make anomalies visible early. The goal is not to build agents that never fail, that’s not realistic. The goal is to build research software that fails gracefully, recovers intelligently, and gives you enough signal to intervene when it matters.

This session is for engineers and researchers who are either already running AI agents in their workflows or actively evaluating whether to. You don’t need to be an ML researcher to follow along . If you care about reliability, reproducibility, and building software that holds up under real conditions, this is for you.

Presentation. Title: Governing Artificial Intelligence in Research Software Engineering

Presenter Name: Samuel Simango

Bio: Samuel Simango is the Senior Manager: Academic Support Services at the Rhodes University Library. In this role he leads the Library’s activities which support the University’s teaching and learning, research as well as community engagement initiatives. He is also the Deputy Chairperson of Rhodes University’s Human Research Ethics Committee. In this role he has been instrumental in aligning the HREC’s governance documents and practices with Research Data Management principles as well as the responsible use of Artificial Intelligence in research. Samuel’s area of specialisation is in the conceptualisation and implementation of Research Data Management systems – particularly insofar as this relates to the integration of lifecycle models, governance frameworks, technological solutions and services.

Abstract: The deployment of Generative Artificial Intelligence tools such as Large Language Models (LLMs) within Research Software Engineering (RSE) significantly accelerates scientific software production. Recent empirical evaluations of AI-assisted Research Software Engineering demonstrate a rapid acceleration in AI-driven scientific code generation. While contemporary RSE research optimises these tools for software maturity, institutional policies routinely fail to address the systemic data privacy risks which this introduces. Using cloud-based AI tools introduces severe data governance vulnerabilities, particularly regarding compliance with data protection laws.

As AI tools become ubiquitous in academia, higher education institutions face an urgent need to ensure responsible research integrity. However, a significant gap exists between high-level ethical policies and practical software development. While many institutional strategies rely on a priori assumptions that governance documents alone suffice, true accountability requires technical oversight.

This presentation addresses this critical regulatory gap by introducing an actionable Institutional AI-RSE Policy Compliance Checklist. This checklist serves as a practical, step-by-step governance tool tailored for South African research facilities, enabling administrators and researchers to systematically mitigate legal risks without stifling technological innovation. Attendees will learn how to deploy this administrative tool to establish a clear, auditable link between institutional data protection mandates and daily research software engineering practices.

Specifically, this session details how the compliance checklist operationalises a four-pillared policy framework:

  1. Governance and Tool Vetting: Vetting AI vendor terms to enforce Zero Data Retention and compliance with the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA).

  2. Data Tiering and Risk Classification: Mapping research data types - from public repositories to critical POPIA special personal information - to permissible AI processing environments.

  3. Input Sanitisation and Prompt Hygiene: Executing protocols to eliminate direct identifiers, metadata, and connection strings from code.

  4. Local Infrastructure Activation: Setting policy criteria that mandate a transition to offline, air-gapped open-source LLMs hosted on-premises.

Presentation. Title: The eLwazi (DSI) Platform: AI-Assisted Data Harmonization and Metadata Curation for Multi-Site Health Research in Africa

Presenter Name: Lyndon Zass

Bio: Lyndon Zass is a University of Cape Town-based geneticist and data specialist whose work bridges human genetics, public health, and bioinformatics to advance phenotype standardisation and genomic medicine across the African research landscape. Lyndon Zass serves as a Project Manager African Genomics Data Hub (AfriGen-D). In addition, he plays a substantial role in the eLwazi Open Data Science Platform (ODSP) as a Project Co ordinator and co chair of the eLwazi Data Support Work Package.

Abstract: The Data Science for Health Discovery and Innovation in Africa (DS-I Africa) consortium generates diverse health data spanning genomics, epidemiology, climate, clinical studies, and public health across African research hubs. However, effective cross-project collaboration faces major barriers: inconsistent metadata standards limit dataset discovery, heterogeneous data collection practices prevent variable alignment across sites, divergent access policies and institutional reluctance restrict data sharing, and dispersed teams have difficulty providing remote harmonization support with limited familiarity with AI-assisted platforms. We aimed to develop AI-assisted data harmonization infrastructure that enables interoperability across DS-I Africa, providing researchers with accessible tools for data discovery, metadata standardization, and variable harmonization while maintaining data sovereignty and adhering to international standards. As part of the eLwazi Open Data Science Platform, we implemented a REDCap-based Metadata Catalogue and an AI-assisted Data Harmonization Tool. The catalogue serves as a central repository with secure access controls, standardized templates, and API synchronization to a public interface. The harmonization tool integrates locally hosted and SaaS Large Language Models to accelerate variable alignment and ontology mapping through intelligent suggestions. The Catalogue currently aggregates metadata from projects across multiple African countries, extending beyond the DS-I Africa consortium to include initiatives like Data Science Without Borders. It provides searchable access to demographic, health, omic, medical imaging, and geospatial data. Presentation. Title: Wrong Search, Right Career: Rethinking How We Attract Research Infrastructure Engineers

Presenter Name: Ijeoma Ezika

Bio: Ijeoma Ezika is a Senior Research Infrastructure Engineer at the University of Southampton, supporting HPC and research computing infrastructure across disciplines. She holds a PhD in electrical and electronics engineering and is a Royal Academy of Engineering and TechWomen Fellow. Prior to her current role, she lectured and conducted engineering education research in Nigeria, funded by a Royal Academy of Engineering’s Engineering Skills Where They Are Most Needed grant.

At Southampton, she led the institutional deployment of Open OnDemand, recognised as a finalist in the Making it Happen category at the Vice-Chancellor’s Awards. Her work spans HPC systems administration, infrastructure deployment, monitoring, and researcher support across the University.

Abstract: Research Infrastructure Engineering is a growing profession that plays a central role in enabling research, yet it remains poorly visible as a deliberate career destination — even among doctoral researchers who have spent years developing directly relevant skills. A snapshot of job sites globally reveals the same role advertised under at least nine different titles across UK universities: HPC Systems Administrator, Research Operations Engineer, HPC Cloud Engineer, HPC DevOps Engineer, Research IT Platform Engineer, HPC and Research Data Systems Engineer, Research Computing Systems Engineer, Research Infrastructure Developer, and HPC and AI Infrastructure Engineer. One institution noted explicitly that their advertised title was “for advertising purposes only” — the appointed candidate would hold a different title entirely. Without a consistent name, a qualified candidate has no reliable search term to start with.

While some community scaffolding exists — UKRI’s Digital Research Infrastructure programme being one example — Research Infrastructure Engineering has not yet developed the shared professional identity that Research Software Engineering has built over more than a decade: a common name, dedicated conferences, national associations, and active career outreach. The field is consequently filled largely through accidental discovery rather than deliberate career choice.

This has practical consequences. Engineers who arrive from a research background bring a specific kind of insight to infrastructure work — the ability to identify systemic access barriers from the inside. This perspective has demonstrable impact: in one case study presented here, it informed the deployment of a browser-based HPC access platform now serving hundreds of users at a UK institution, and a documentation overhaul that addressed long-standing assumptions of prior knowledge that were quietly excluding less experienced researchers.

This talk presents the evidence, situates it within what the literature tells us about RSE career entry points, and opens a conversation about what it would take to make research infrastructure careers genuinely findable.

Presentation. Title: Training the Trainers: Community-Led Bioinformatics Training with Bioconductor Across Africa

Presenter Name: Laurah Nyasita Ondari

Bio: Laurah Ondari is a bioinformatician based at the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) at the ILRI campus in Nairobi, Kenya, where her work focuses on genomics, transcriptomics, and reproducible bioinformatics workflows. Abstract: Community-led training, local instructor development, and open educational resources are increasingly important for sustaining computational research capacity and reproducible analysis practices in under-resourced settings. Through Bioconductor, an open-source research software ecosystem for biological data analysis, training activities across Africa have focused not only on introducing researchers to bioinformatics workflows but also on supporting local teaching capacity and creating pathways for continued skills development. Although high-throughput sequencing technologies are increasingly used across Africa, access to structured and advanced bioinformatics training remains limited, restricting the adoption of reproducible genomic analysis workflows across many institutions.

Through a Chan Zuckerberg Initiative-funded project, Bioconductor expanded coordinated bioinformatics training activities across East and West Africa in 2025. Bioconductor also began developing a specialised training roadmap for 2026–2027, including online seminars, advanced workshops co-designed with regional partners, and clearer pathways linking introductory and advanced training.

These approaches may also be useful for similar capacity-building efforts in other regions where access to advanced bioinformatics and research software training remains limited.

Presentation. Title: Bridging the Gap: Practical Approaches to Reproducible Bioinformatics Workflows in Low-Resource Settings

Presenter Name: Lilian Oluchi Nwagbara

Bio: Lilian Oluchi Nwagbara is a biomedical science and bioinformatics professional from Enugu, Nigeria. She has hands‑on experience in molecular biology techniques, genomic data analysis, and computational workflows. Lilian has worked as a Research Associate at CompChem Hub and the Floret Center for Advanced Genomics & Bioinformatics Research, supporting variant analysis, workflow troubleshooting, and training delivery. Skilled in tools such as Galaxy, Nextflow, and MEGA, she has applied them in real‑world projects to improve accessibility and reliability of bioinformatics pipelines. Beyond research, she has contributed to national data quality initiatives and facilitated digital literacy training. Passionate about scientific communication and community engagement, Lilian is committed to advancing bioinformatics research across Africa.

Abstract: In bioinformatics, reproducibility often assumes access to high-performance computing, stable internet connectivity, and uninterrupted power supply. Yet for many researchers in African contexts, these assumptions rarely hold. Consequently, this assumption creates a critical barrier: workflows that are technically sound in theory but impractical across low-resource settings. This talk draws on first-hand experience working on genomic datasets in Nigeria to show how reproducibility can still be achieved despite these constraints through three strategies: containerized workflows, cloud-based platforms, and offline tools.

First, containerized workflows with Nextflow and Apptainer or Docker efficiently manage code and dependencies, ensuring portability and reliability even in the face of connectivity challenges. Second, web- and cloud-platforms like Galaxy create collaborative, user-friendly environments that enable researchers to run complex pipelines without requiring powerful local hardware. Third, workflow execution can be augmented by incorporating offline tools like MEGA, ensuring progress even in bandwidth-restricted settings.

Comparing these strategies shifts the focus of bioinformatics research reproducibility from resource availability to intentional workflow design and tool choice. Moving beyond assumptions of high-resource research settings, this talk highlights practical techniques for reproducibility which attendees can apply to their unique research environments, proving that reliable science can be conducted anywhere. This perspective directly aligns with the RSAfrica26 theme, “Research Software Without Borders.”

Workshop. Title: Meta-valuation Across Borders: A Cross-Community Experiment in Valuing Diverse Research Contributions

Presenter Name: Dr Cooper Smout

Bio: Dr. Cooper Smout is a designer, neuroscientist, and open science entrepreneur working at the intersection of collective intelligence, participatory governance, and cultural change. With a background in architecture and a PhD in the neuroscience of consciousness, he left academia to found Free Our Knowledge, a collective action platform for open research, and Open Heart & Mind (OHM), a nonprofit organisation developing open-source tools to empower communities. His current work focuses on meta-valuation, a participatory evaluation framework that generates transparent, interoperable value metrics to support fair recognition and reward, decentralised coordination, and collective governance across research and commons-oriented ecosystems.

Abstract: In this workshop we’ll explore meta-valuation, a novel mechanism for valuing diverse research contributions, and provide a proof-of-concept for its capacity to foster coordination and incentivise cross-border collaboration through interoperable value metrics. Its key innovation is a self-referential evaluation process, in which any contribution — including evaluations themselves — can be valued in relative terms, placing diverse research contributions on a common scale and aligning incentives toward the common good.

At RSAA25, we conducted a live pilot test that produced relative valuations for diverse conference contributions in real-time, spanning presentations, financial sponsorship, facilitation, and behind-the-scenes efforts. Since then, a full deployment at OHM Gathering 2025 demonstrated the mechanism’s capacity to coordinate and recognise contributions across a multi-day community event. Together, these experiments demonstrate in principle that the same mechanism can value all forms of contribution — labour, capital, and ideas — through interoperable metrics tied to collective values. We now aim to take the next step: demonstrating interoperability in practice across a pair of workshops at RSAA26 and RSAfrica26.

Each workshop will begin with a brief introduction to the mechanism and key findings. Participants will then nominate and approve contributions for review via a web-app interface, before collectively valuing contributions using a simple pairwise choice between randomly assigned contributions. The app will aggregate these votes into a relative value for each contribution and a total for each contributor. Crucially, both workshops will include local contributions — such as presentations — and global contributions — such as the common website infrastructure — enabling comparison and exploration of the mechanism’s interoperability across contexts. This will demonstrate a key property not yet evidenced: that recognition metrics can accumulate across communities who adopt the same evaluation dimensions, incentivising contributors to make their outputs openly available.

We’ll close by celebrating the key contributors our data suggests made each conference happen, and reflecting on the process and results. Following both workshops, we will compare datasets, analyse findings, and aim to publish as a proof of concept for cross-community interoperability, with credit given to all contributors through the same mechanism explored within.
